What the water actually sees when a fuse is at pump angle

A fuselage only sits parallel to the flow in a drawing. In flight it is pitched nose-up, and at that angle the cut the water crosses is a long thin aerofoil with roughly six times the chord of the fuse height. Its shape is the fuse's own cross-section outline, stretched along the chord, which makes the corner radii an aerofoil decision rather than a cosmetic one.

How the slice is computed

Put the fuse axis along x with the nose at x=0, lateral across the fuse as y, and vertical as z. With the fuse pitched nose-up by α, the oncoming water runs along V = V(cosα, 0, sinα).

A streamwise plane is any plane containing that vector. For a fuse that is tall and narrow the flow is essentially two-dimensional in the planes that cut across the thin direction, the same way flow over a mast is two-dimensional in horizontal planes. Those planes are −x sinα + z cosα = d. Using chordwise coordinate u = x cosα + z sinα and lateral v = y, the half-thickness of the section is

t(u)/2 = (w(x)/2) · Φ(ζ), with ζ = 2z/h(x), x = u cosα − d sinα, z = u sinα + d cosα.

Φ is the fuse's own cross-section outline: half-width as a fraction of the maximum, plotted against normalised height. That is the whole point. Because ζ runs linearly from −1 to +1 along the cut, the streamwise aerofoil's thickness distribution is the fuse cross-section outline, stretched out along the chord. A square-cornered fuse gives a rectangle. An elliptical fuse gives an ellipse. If you want the flow to see a proper aerofoil, the fuse cross-section has to be that aerofoil, stood on end.

The chord follows c ≈ h / sinα, so at 10° a 38 mm fuse presents a 219 mm chord. At α = 0 the cut degenerates to the horizontal plan section of chord L, which is the familiar picture, and the model passes through that case continuously.

How the drag is estimated

Two independent estimates run side by side, because neither alone deserves much trust.

Headline number. Every parallel cut across the body is built, each is given a section drag coefficient from flat-plate skin friction with a Hoerner strut form factor 1 + 2(t/c) + 60(t/c)⁴ applied to the real wetted perimeter, plus separate blunt leading-edge and blunt base terms proportional to the cut-off thickness there. Those are integrated across the body to give the force. Seawater at ν = 1.05×10⁻⁶ m²/s, ρ = 1025 kg/m³.

Section solve. The selected cut is run through a constant-strength source panel method for the inviscid surface velocity, then a Thwaites laminar boundary layer, Michel transition, and Head's entrainment method for the turbulent part, with Squire–Young for the profile drag. This is where separation shows up.

Both are two-dimensional strip estimates. They ignore the junctions at each end, the tail's downforce, the front wing's downwash, and all three-dimensional relief, so absolute numbers are indicative. Differences between shapes at the same angle are the useful output.

How the optimiser works

Minimising drag on its own is meaningless, because it just drives the fuse to zero width. So two things are held at least as good as the fuse you already have: vertical bending stiffness Iyy = w·h³·I₂/8, which is what resists the tail's downforce, and cross-sectional area A = w·h·I₀/2, standing in for weight. I₀ and I₂ are integrals of the outline function, so they capture shape as well as size.

The stiffness floor sets the thinnest allowable width at any height, w_min(h) = 8·Iyy*/(h³·I₂), and the area ceiling sets the tallest useful height. Everything between those is a valid design, so each candidate outline is walked across that range rather than assuming the point where the two constraints meet is the answer. Height is capped at 55 mm for what the mast and wing joints will take.

Read the margin, not the winner. The objective only sees thickness ratio, wetted area and how blunt the ends are. It separates clean outlines from blunt ones decisively and can barely tell two clean outlines apart. A plain ellipse currently wins from every starting point with a designed aerofoil section within about 1%, and that 1% is inside the model's noise rather than a result. The large margins against square-edged bars are real.

Lateral stiffness is reported but not constrained. A tall thin blade gives it up quickly, and the optimiser is free to spend it.

What the pump model assumes

The fuse angle relative to the water is θ − γ, which is also the front wing's angle of attack. So it is not a free choice: it is whatever the lift equation demands at your speed and loading. That is the whole point. Slow, high-lift pumping is exactly where the fuse is worst aligned.

Trim comes from Cl = 2W/(ρSV²) with a lift slope of 2π/(1 + 2/AR), clamped at a 13° stall. Cross-checked against compute_trim_angle in foil-rl-pump/python-rl/foil_env/foil_physics.py in this repo, which solves the full wing, stabiliser and mast balance and gives 8.95° at 3.5 m/s and 5.44° at 4.5 m/s. This reimplements the wing term only and lands within 2.5%.

On top of trim, the pump swings the flight path by atan(A·ω/V), which is large: 80 mm of heave at 2 Hz and 4.5 m/s is ±12.6°. The rider feathers most of that out by pitching with the stroke. The feathering slider is that fraction, and it is the one number here with no independent source behind it.

The heave is assumed sinusoidal and the feathering constant through the stroke. Driving this from a trained policy rollout would be more honest than either assumption.
